# Episode 2 — Carol Xu: Following a Path That Makes No Sense on Paper

**Guest:** Carol Xu, founder of Reimagine Livelihood and the Relational Dojo
**Host:** Shaun Button
**Recorded:** 2026

---

**Shaun:** Hey Carol, it's great to see you, and thanks for coming on. How are you arriving today?

**Carol:** Hi Shaun. Yeah, happy to see you. It's been a year since we met in person near Toronto. How am I? Feeling pretty good overall. My hands and feet are always cold. And my husband likes to keep the room cold. He likes cold and I don't. So. Yeah, that's me. How are you?

**Shaun:** I'm doing well. Yeah, it's Canada, so I suffer the cold quite a bit too. Happy to be here — great to connect with you again. So where I want to start is: who were you before arriving in this kind of liminal space?

**Carol:** Yeah, it's funny — I just shared that with two other people an hour ago. Looking back, Kierkegaard said life only makes sense when you look back. When you're living it, it doesn't make sense. But now I look back, and there's a very clear watershed moment for me.

It was when I was writing my PhD dissertation. I just basically started to have a breakdown — internally. On the outside, things looked fine. But from the inside, I had this high ambition of writing a groundbreaking dissertation, and I was ready to devote my life to academia. When I finished my PhD, my plan was to become a professor — to study the theory of organizing, the sociology and psychology of work and organizations.

That was the first thirty years of my life — I was always in some kind of academic setting. That was what I thought my life was going to be.

Until I started having heightened anxiety, feelings of inadequacy, comparing myself to the big-shot professors in the field — what they had done, what I could ever do. My dissertation progress just stalled. I was forced to look within. I was living at Berkeley at the time. I tried psychotherapy. Then my intuition told me: try meditation. So I explored different schools, started meditating. None of that offers a quick fix, of course. So it was a few gruesome years. Doubt — doubting my whole worth, my whole existence. I was on the edge of an anxiety disorder or clinical depression. I was never diagnosed, but definitely felt up and down a lot every day.

But that long story short: it really forced me to look at something I hadn't looked at before — mostly things within. And then, slowly, very slowly, I started to see things differently. Thanks to my meditation teacher — mostly Buddhist traditions — I started to connect with how different wisdom traditions around the world are actually all pointing at very similar things. That was sixteen years ago. That started a new stage of life. Yeah. I'll follow your questions from here.

**Shaun:** Yeah. Sounds like a difficult but meaningful period. So I'm curious — what happened with school? Were you trying to continue on that path while healing?

**Carol:** Yeah. That was really difficult. The good thing is I had a lot of time — no coursework, just dissertation. When dissertation isn't coming along, you have all the time in the world. But the stories from that period are not helpful ones.

At one point I thought: they might just kick me out of the program. And if so, so be it. I'll focus on what I can do right now, in this moment. All that future orientation — wanting to be this big-shot professor — got pushed out. What I started learning from meditation was to focus on this moment. What can I do for my dissertation? And at the beginning, I could do very, very little. Like, let me just read one interview. I do fieldwork for my research — let me just process one at a time.

It felt trivial. But part of me said: the other approach isn't working. Let me try something that doesn't feel like it's going to work, but that feels right. And let me just focus on that. It was a complete shift of approach — a paradigm shift — that helped me slowly become productive again.

I also got completely burned out from the anxiety and everything. And then — this sounds crazy — I was like, I want to be a parent. I'd gotten married at that time. Originally I thought I'd defer everything, finish the PhD, get a job, and then have a kid. But during that period I thought: no, I don't want to wait. I don't know if I'll ever finish the PhD or get an academic job. I'll just have a kid first.

My husband and I decided to have a child while I was still writing the dissertation. It actually gave me a break from thinking about the work. I just focused on having a kid, on being a mother. The first year of being a mother was also really hard — almost its own kind of depression. But it gave part of me a complete rest — the part that worried about my work, my identity, my future.

And after a year of focusing on the child, my whole perspective on the dissertation had shifted. I realized: I had such high standards on myself. It doesn't need to be that. And then I finished my dissertation, got my degree.

But after I got my degree, I started to see the accumulation of habits in me — the future orientation, the status focus, all of those things. I thought: since I've internalized these habits, if I stay in academia, those habits will only get entrenched. I'm not saying academia is inherently bad — it's just that for me, personally, I didn't want to continue in an environment where my old habits would keep being strengthened. That was the main reason I decided not to get a job in academia. And then I started just exploring ever since. A non-beaten path.

**Shaun:** Yeah. This sounds familiar. I call it a dark night of the soul — that period of anxiety, borderline depression. Almost a kind of madness. But there come insights from that.

**Carol:** Mm-hmm.

**Shaun:** And there's a searching for paths that have been followed that can provide some guidance. During this time — what did you find that helped? You mentioned meditation, but were there thinkers, stories, other practices that really helped you through this?

**Carol:** Yeah. Meeting my meditation teacher definitely helped. I walked into an in-person class at the Berkeley Buddhist Monastery one Wednesday, and I was in that class from that point forward. I didn't expect that — it just happened.

Thinking back, I think that for big moments in life, what helped me most was intuition. The intuition that this is not working. If I continue to live this way, I might still be able to become a tenured professor, check all the boxes — but this is exhausting my life. That intuition was quite clear. And that there has to be a different way. That was also quite clear. I just didn't know exactly what that way was. It was like feeling in the dark, following my intuition.

After meeting my meditation teacher, I started encountering other teachers from different traditions — on YouTube, online. And there's a more and more clear internal gauge: yes, that resonates. That's right. But there was also a trial-and-error process of learning how to trust my intuition. And developing companionships and friendships with people who would check each other — agree to point out blind spots for one another. That also helped.

**Shaun:** I'm wondering about the conflict between the rational mind and intuition. When intuition comes up, I check it with rationality, and it doesn't always pass. But somehow, trusting it seems to work out better. At least that's what I'm still experimenting with. So did you find resistance to the intuition? How do you learn to trust it, especially coming from a very rational background?

**Carol:** Yeah. The intuition, through trial and error, has come to the foreground now — meaning I'm very aware of it. And looking back, throughout childhood, intuition was always guiding me. I just never gave it the credit or thought of myself that way.

I was taught to be rational. I was pretty good at school throughout. So that rational framework is always there. And it's useful — it helps with operations management, time management, all of that. But if that whole framework is devoid of organic life, it's also what drove me into anxiety and depression. It rings out the vitality of life.

I think of a quote — somebody attributed it to Einstein — something about how the heart is what connects us to the sacred, to the priceless. And the mind is the rational part. But in today's society, we've flipped it. The heart is supposed to be the master and the mind the servant, but we've enslaved the heart to serve the rational mind. Both are useful. Both can complement each other. But if the relationship is flipped, that leads to a lot of burnout and violence, and ironically becomes deeply irrational.

**Shaun:** Yeah. You lose the meaning. You're just doing it because it makes sense, because it's optimal or efficient or what you're told to do.

**Carol:** Yeah. Yeah.

**Shaun:** Yeah, that reminds me of Iain McGilchrist — the heart as the right brain, the mind as the left brain — and how often they're flipped. I'm trying to do that flip myself right now. I know it's the way to go, but it's hard to do all the time, especially in a rational society.

**Carol:** Yeah, that's where we can be honest with ourselves and with each other, however much we can. However much we shift counts. We don't have to get there overnight.

**Shaun:** Okay, so let's go back to the timeline. You have a child, you've finished your dissertation. How do you go about this new way?

**Carol:** Yeah. I finished my dissertation about twelve years ago now. So I have a teenager. After finishing and not getting an academic job, it was actually a few more years of existential crisis, because I had been in an academic institution ever since I was six. Primary school all the way through, never missing a beat — through two or three different graduate schools. I was always on a path, and being status-oriented, the status of that world was also pretty much my identity.

And now: who am I? I'm nobody. I have a PhD, but people always look at what's next. The past is never good enough. That was quite difficult, in a very different way.

But the contemplative perspective I'd developed started to kick in — in a real way. It's no longer just "I went to a meditation class, and that's it." By now, new habits had started to take root, new perspectives and viewpoints. In some ways, it forced me to shed a big part of the ego. And when you have a lighter ego, you're actually more resilient. It's okay to be a nobody.

By now, the depression and anxiety had significantly subsided. And I started to realize something about depression itself: in depression, my mind constructed a world in which I was the only loser — no matter how other people saw me. People might think, "Oh my God, you have a PhD and a kid and you're married, you're succeeding." But the nature of depression is that it's not about those so-called objective realities. It's about the subjective reality that somehow clicked with the narratives of inadequacy, and weaved everything into a world in which I was the only loser. That explains why a person feels isolated and meaningless and worthless.

Once I had that understanding — thanks to meditation and everything — it was so much easier to deal with the darkness. Because I could see it for what it was.

Very practically: whenever I started to feel doubt and darkness and loneliness — oh my god, I'm not on this typical path, my peers are already assistant professors at Harvard and MIT, meanwhile who am I — there would be a moment of popping up, stepping out. And just: oh, okay. Yeah, I'm having those feelings. That's familiar. And then checking: oh, actually, I'm exhausted. It's late in the evening. These scary thoughts might be true, but I don't need to act on them right now. I can just go sleep. I don't need to sweep them under the carpet, but let me get some rest and I'll come back and look at it. And then 99%, even 100% of the time, after I sleep and rest and come back — I don't feel exactly the same way. That was such a valuable learning from the depression period.

**Shaun:** Right. That's the power of meditation — it allows you to step back from your thoughts, separate yourself from them, observe them, understand what's going on. In depression especially, it's helpful. It doesn't become who you are. It becomes a thought that you have.

**Carol:** Yeah.

**Shaun:** And you can get to the root of it — oh, I'm just tired, this can pass, I'm just stressed out, that's why this is happening. It's not who I am, and I don't have to do something about this right now.

**Carol:** Yeah. And meditation isn't just a technique for sitting on a cushion — it's a way of life. That process can come online while you're feeling depressed or feeling any kind of emotion, without needing to carve out five minutes. It just becomes available.

**Shaun:** Yeah, so at this period — it sounds like you've pretty much emptied yourself out. You don't have your identity, you don't have your community. When do you start rebuilding those things?

**Carol:** That will come later. The emptying is really — it can be painful, and it was painful, but it's also really helpful.

What you're asking about community — that was, I would say, beginning of the pandemic. Before the pandemic, I kept my professional path and my spiritual or contemplative path separate. The contemplative thing was very private. It gave me so much nourishment and power, but I didn't really tell people about it, especially in my professional life. And the professional life was just the professional life.

When the pandemic happened, my professional projects came to a pause. I was helping a social impact startup do time banking. That whole thread got paused — no funding, the CEO didn't know if they could continue. Part of me was in panic, of course. But there was also a voice — coming back to intuition again — just a feeling: wow, this is unprecedented. This might open a door to something. I don't know what. And I was like: okay, where would this lead?

Looking back — that's how I connected with people around the world. Before that, I had collaborated with people, started a company with people, worked at several different jobs. But I kind of felt alone. Very alone. Because my meditation teacher's teaching can be quite esoteric — it's not the popular, accessible Buddhism or meditation. So I was like: okay, this contemplative path has nourished me so much, but I don't know how many people in the whole world understand it. That's why I kept it private. I was one person at work and another person... elsewhere.

That was how I felt all the way until the pandemic. Then when it happened, I started going on Zoom and joining meetings starting in Germany, in Southeast Asia. I started meeting people from all over the world. And I'd go into their communities and hear them talk and think: oh, actually, there is resonance with the contemplative path I'm on. Wow, you also talk that way. You also use that word that way. You also care about embodiment.

I just felt greatly encouraged. So I started a learning circle called Reimagine Livelihood. It's a peer-supported learning circle that asks the question: if I want to integrate my professional path and my contemplative path in an honest way, can I actually make a living? That was the impetus.

I invited a former colleague and a couple of people I'd met online. They brought in a couple of people. A circle of seven. We met once a week for seven weeks and started exploring our relationship to money, to livelihood, to wealth. And by the end, some people were saying: "Wow, I've never shared anything about my trauma with money with anyone before — not even my girlfriend." And wow, we can actually honestly share these things, talk about money. Why don't we try to generate livelihood together, now that we can talk about it this way?

After that learning circle, most of us stayed to keep experimenting. What are our strengths? How might we actually integrate — be more authentic, bring our professional life and our more intimate inner life into alignment? Not necessarily in the way I had done it, but to find overlap. Some of us were joking about starting what someone called a "Buddhist company" or a monastery company.

That later gave rise to what we called the Communication Dojo, which is now the Relational Dojo. And Starter Cultures. And a bunch of online experiments and collaborations. We basically started coming together every Wednesday for two years or so, and that led to a collective consulting practice, and yeah — to a lot of the projects I'm still working on.

**Shaun:** And how did it feel during this process, as you're trying to unite these two paths, finding new communities? What's the felt sense of it? Is it uplifting? Are you finally feeling like you're building something of an authentic self?

**Carol:** It's a new life — from the inside out. I think that period of emptying was really helpful, to force me to let go of a lot of things. Not that I've now completely let go of the ego — I think we'll always have one. But to feel the pain of something I used to hold on to so tightly, like the kind of status I wanted — to be forced to let that go, to experience that pain — then I have space and appreciation for what comes in.

And so now, I feel very, very alive. All of those things just... I just feel really alive from that first Reimagine Livelihood circle, from the people I met.

I think once I allowed myself to be more authentic, more real, the people I met reinforced that. I never saw myself as intentionally fake — my mom hates fake people and can smell one a mile away, and I've never wanted to be that. But I think now that I can integrate the two paths, I can be more authentic, and the people I meet reinforce that in a kind of loop.

So I feel more ease in my life and my work. The people I collaborate with now — we all agree to practice relational skills, to be straightforward about our shadows, to practice giving and receiving money honestly. All of that makes life personally easier and easier. Easier to surface conflict. It's not that I don't have conflict anymore. I still feel exhaustion and tiredness. But somehow overall it just feels more and more useful. Does that make sense?

**Shaun:** Yeah. I get two feelings from it. One is the feeling of holding onto conflict and not resolving it — I'm very conflict-avoidant, so I held a lot. I get that tense feeling coming up. And yeah, the ease when somebody is more open to discussing things without it boiling over.

The other thing that comes up is my first time meeting you. I think it was Limicon — just seeing you present. And afterwards I was like: there's something about Carol. I want to reach out and work with her, though I didn't know on what. There's the intelligence you can sense, the competence, but also the authenticity and openness. And then meeting you in person — I felt it even more. You were so caring, making sure I was all right, had everything I needed, checking in on the kids. It's really comforting. Your way of being comes across. It's hard to pinpoint, but I think people are drawn to it. At least I was.

**Carol:** Yeah. What you just named is actually a characteristic of different paradigms. In the dominant rational paradigm, you have to be able to articulate value. I want to spend time with this person because they have power, money, whatever. You can see it in a relatively visible way.

In this other paradigm — what I call a heart-centered paradigm — a lot of things you have to experience. In the rational paradigm, people sell things: "you should come to my program, take my workshop, work with me, give me money" — rational selling. My product will save you time and money.

In this other paradigm, most things of value you have to experience. You can't just put them on paper. That's why intuition is not just thin intuition — intuitive power is huge in this paradigm, and it can be cultivated more and more.

And I'm drawn to this person — sometimes because I grew up in a certain way and that pattern matches, so it's not always that I'm drawn to something "good." But there is so much in this paradigm that is not explicitly written, not legible. It's so needed to listen in a different way, to attune to the subtle signals.

Because life itself is mostly silent. Other than this voice — my breathing, my heart rate, how I absorb nutrients and discharge them — most of what's going on happens in silence. It's the majority of who we are. And most of it comes through experience.

That's also why people in this paradigm, when we try to communicate to people mostly in the rational paradigm, the communication is very hard. "Why did you leave your job to do relational community work?" On paper it does not look good at all. There is no rational way of telling them.

But for this paradigm, what is key is cultivating our intuitive ability — sensing each other, attuning to the subtle relationships between people, sensing whether somebody is lying, and not just judging them for lying but noticing: maybe that person is lying because there is fear behind it. Actually helping that person connect to the fear. That is mostly invisible work. And it's so key to helping us thrive.

**Shaun:** Right, yeah. So that explains it. It seems like you've now tried to flip your work and integrate it. It's bringing that intuitive world to people so they can experience it. But you still have to do that in the rational world and make a living. So how is that going? How's your experience of working in this new way?

**Carol:** It's harder than I thought when I first started Reimagine Livelihood. At the very beginning I was like: this is risky. Can I actually tell the world I want to infuse a more contemplative approach into consulting, into helping organizations grow and develop? Before the pandemic, the professional path was: use the resume, I have a PhD, here you go. And now if I want to integrate the more spiritual — this paradigm — will people actually trust me and pay me for services?

That's why I started the peer-supported learning circle. And I thought: I want to take the risk. I'm still young. I want to give it a shot. See whether I can make a living from this. During the pandemic, things had stopped anyway. So I said: let me just use this vacuum to experiment. If I can start making okay money in a year or two, I'll keep doing this. If not, maybe I'll go get a nine-to-five job.

Actually, there's something that was really valuable — I learned this from a person named Audrey at Service Space, a community I find so innovative and so heart-centered. Audrey was a full-time volunteer when I met her, working on many different projects. I was like: wow, is that for real? How can a person do that? She's younger than me.

I got to talk to her one-on-one. I asked: how do you do that? She said: "I don't recommend this to anyone — it only worked for me so far." I asked: how do you pay rent? She said: "I minimize my expenses and I have some savings. And if the savings run out, I'll get a job. Actually, before this full-time volunteering, I went back to teach elementary school. I did that, then had some savings, didn't need income right away, all these educational projects started popping up at Service Space, I was interested, so I ended up doing this full time. But I can always get a job if I need to."

Just hearing her say that — matter of fact, without fear or concern, without saying it was laudable or what I should do. It was just: this happened to me, this is what I can do in this moment. That stayed with me ever since. That was a reality-shattering moment. Another letting go of: this has to be a career, this is what you do, this is where your security comes from.

Hey — you can let all of that go. And if the savings run out, you can get a job. I used to think oh, with my resume I need to get paid a certain way. Now I think: yeah, if my savings run out, I could hide the resume and be a cashier at Safeway if they'll have me. Like — yeah, I'll get some kind of work if it comes to that.

That was the thought behind: I'll allow myself to experiment with a new way of livelihood for a year or two and then re-evaluate. Time flew by. It's now been six or seven years since the pandemic. And I'll be honest — I still haven't made enough money to pay the mortgage for my family on my own. It's still very unstable and very little. But I do have some savings. And the way I think about it is: this is a tremendous privilege, that I have some savings. So I want to use it to keep experimenting and building — building infrastructure, building scaffold — so that more and more people doing heart-centered work can have more stable livelihood.

**Shaun:** Right — supporting other people on a similar path.

**Carol:** In the path that they want. Yeah. And also — the way we think about money. For example, in the Relational Dojo, when we allocate donations, we do what we call a Happy Money Story process that also incorporates people's needs into the conversation. There are so many different ways of relating to money. I want to explore that and share what I've learned, so that people can process money in a way that actually helps them thrive, rather than draining relationships or draining communities.

**Shaun:** So what's happening now? And what do you want to see in the future, for you and for these experiments?

**Carol:** I just had a meditation retreat in July. And there's always little shifts in life — that's how the liminal space works. How can you live it more and more, rather than just think about it or talk about it? There are always shifts going on. In July, I felt a deeper shift. It's subtle to describe, so I won't try to fully describe it. But in another way: I have a sense that that shift changed everything.

I want to double down more on this paradigm, in a more heart-centered way, with less fear. I'm now starting a couple of new initiatives, building on the old ones. I just feel there is a more organic energy that wants to be poured through me into these projects. I'm quite excited.

And I also don't want to work in the old way — getting burned out, worrying about future outcomes. So it's also a way of working more organically, seeing everything as a living system. I'm a living system. You are. A team is. An organization is. A network, a community. How can we let more nourishment into our systems, and let that channel into good work? That's where I'm heading.

**Shaun:** Yeah, so it sounds like a feeling of reassurance that you're on the right path, and more energy to do what you're currently doing.

**Carol:** You can see it that way, yeah. But I guess it's a kind of reassurance I cannot give to myself. It's not from willpower, not from the thinking mind. It's not: "Carol, you're doing a great job." It's a reassurance that just appeared. And then more clarity about where I want to put myself and how I want to show up. It's not something I can talk myself into through rational reasoning. It's not an ego boost. It's something else. The effect of it is pretty much what you said, but it's broader. I'll put it that way.

**Shaun:** Right — something you can't put words to. But I'm trying.

**Carol:** Yeah, thank you. I appreciate that. Maybe it was also a bigger vision — a sense of what may be possible. So. Yeah.

**Shaun:** Yeah. That's it. I'm trying to imagine that sort of message coming in, almost feeling external to you. It sounds like a powerful message you received, wherever it came from. So — how is that materializing? This is your chance to pitch what you're working on. What do you have happening?

**Carol:** I have quite a few things. Someone recently invited me to apply for a fellowship, so I actually compiled a list. I can share links afterward — but let me describe a few I'm excited about.

One is the Living Systems Research Initiative. It's brand new. I used to be in academic research, but this kind of research I want to do is so much broader. I want to invite different ways of knowing — how do we learn about living systems? A farm, nature, ecology. How can we learn to attune to living systems? To draw from permaculture, natural farming. And then apply those insights to social systems, to stop treating ourselves and our organizations like machines, and start treating our social systems as part of a larger living system — and also as living systems in their own right.

I want to do research that is intimately connected to application, to experiment, to embodied knowledge — so that people in communities and organizations can actually apply it right away. Does this work? It doesn't work. Let's try a different thing. So that's something I'm starting.

I'm also starting a YouTube channel — haven't made actual content yet — where I'll invite people to talk about their insights about living systems. It might be a permaculture farmer, it might be somebody who's been facilitating community building for years. Both the natural-systems side and the social-systems side. My hope is that actually they're the same — I'm only making a distinction because of how our current culture frames them. I specifically also want to invite organizational leaders who are interested to explore the living systems perspective and apply it to their organization and team.

**Shaun:** Great. Yeah, I'm interested to see where that goes — and to check out the YouTube channel once there's content. Anything else you'd like people to know about?

**Carol:** The Relational Dojo — some folks in Intentional Society are already aware of that. If I can share some links afterward for people to explore after this conversation, that would be great.

There's also an initiative called Reclaim Work, really exploring what human work can be in the age of AI and extractive business models. Actually drawing a lot from wisdom traditions — there's a forgetting of what work was hundreds of years ago. It's not even about inventing new things; it's connecting to something essential to humans that we've forgotten.

There's the Coherence Weaving Initiative — more in organizational and community settings. How do we invite differences into a community, into a network, into an organization? Not trying to resolve conflict as if it's a problem, but treating differences as the precursor of breakthroughs, treating conflict as the precursor of transformation.

And then there's the Village Network Playground — you and I went to a physical gathering last year. That whole crew continues to work together, meeting every week, trying to weave the relationships we're already in into a larger supportive network — beyond any one community, beyond Intentional Society. Based on the relationships we already have. That one is ongoing.

I'll stop there. There are still a couple more. But yeah.

**Shaun:** Well, that was wonderful. I think we can wrap it up there. It's been great having you on, great connecting with you again. Quite a powerful story — I could hear echoes of my own story in it, and I'm sure others will. And it's exciting to see what you're working on now — that redirection of the energy you had when you were an academic, curious and driven, now applied in a new way. It is inspiring. So best of luck with all these ventures and on your path. Thanks again for coming on.

**Carol:** Yeah, thank you so much, Shaun. And now I wish I had talked less and heard more of your echoes.

**Shaun:** Yes. It's always a bit one-sided.

**Carol:** Yeah, but you ask such good questions — I just immediately dropped into my experience.
