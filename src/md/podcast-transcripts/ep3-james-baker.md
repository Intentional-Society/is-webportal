# Episode 3 — James Baker: Devoted

**Guest:** James Baker, founder of Intentional Society
**Host:** Shaun Button
**Recorded:** 2026

---

*James Baker grew up believing he was meant to save the world in the religious sense, and to solve the world as a gifted kid who could figure anything out. For years, that worked. He climbed the career ladder, leading bigger and bigger changes, until one day it didn't. He hit a ceiling, and the stress followed him home. In this conversation, we talk about what came apart, what he found on the other side, and the moment in a small attic yoga studio in Seattle that changed how he saw himself. We get into what he's learning about leadership, and at the end, I asked him to try to name the thing he's devoted to. I came away seeing James and a bit of myself differently. I hope you do, too.*

---

**Shaun:** Hi, James. Thanks for joining today. Let's start with a little check-in. How are you arriving?

**James:** Good morning. Just getting going. Feeling pretty fresh. Glad to be with you.

**Shaun:** It's great to have you here. This is a big one — you're the key person to have on the Intentional Society podcast. The idea is to get a picture of James before Intentional Society. So take us back as early as you want to go. Who were you before all this?

**James:** Who was I? Who am I? Who are any of us really? I feel like the answer is: normal dude. Conventional dude. Pretty typical life. But I suppose probably not typical in the degree of accomplishment. I grew up as one of those gifted kids — including the common gifted kid syndrome of having a lot of expectation and pressure, and a sense of being gonna go save the world.

In my case that came in two dimensions. Save the world in literally the religious sense — evangelical, Pentecostal, fundamentalist-ish Christianity. Go out and save the souls of the world. And then I got labeled as gifted early on in school, so there was a solve the world expectation as well, at least in my own mind. I was either gonna solve the world or save the world — or combine and do both. Those were my formative influences and identity shapers as a kid.

**Shaun:** You're brought up in an intensive religious setting. Did that put expectations on you, and how did those sit with you?

**James:** Intensive in the sense of church twice a week. It was the main social scene, the community, the norms environment I was swimming in. That shaped a lot for me. And then high school in particular was my captain everything, lead everything, win everything era — academics, sports, music, just do all the things. To the point that going into college I had my first wave of: what am I doing all this for? Kick back, relax, reevaluate. I played a lot of computer games in college.

**Shaun:** Was there a specific moment in high school where you started to notice you were doing too much?

**James:** I don't think so. It wasn't the cumulative effect of drum major of the band, winning the academic decathlon, captaining the tennis team. When it got to undergrad it was just less clear how to play the game. I'd gotten very good at playing games and winning the conventional games. And it was less clear: which games do I want to play here? I decided to pick fewer games to try to win in undergrad.

Then I graduated in computer engineering around the dot-com bust and decided to go to grad school partly as a delaying mechanism — don't know what I want to do, but I'll go a few more years.

**Shaun:** Were you still involved in the church at this point?

**James:** Absolutely still there in undergrad, in the more conservative wing. Then I went to grad school and met liberal Christians for the first time. Had my eyes opened that maybe this whole other side of the Christian tradition — maybe they're Christians too. Grad school was my first wave of deconstruction. Tearing everything apart, stripping the tower of beliefs down to the base layer, and then trying to reconstruct it. First wave, then second wave, then third wave, later in my life.

**Shaun:** What was being deconstructed during that first wave?

**James:** Ultimately, my sense that I knew what was going on with the world. I knew how the universe was created, what the purpose of everything is, what my purpose is — that security of a strong, tight, all-encompassing worldview that accounts for everything. Tearing that down led to a sense of really radical drift and unknowing. If all of this doesn't fit together, then what even is anything? A radical sense of unknowing came up for the first time.

**Shaun:** I feel like that's present for me now. I never had a religious upbringing — it was all science. That led to the game mindset, like you said: everything can be figured out and optimized. I held onto that for a long time, and I'm still in the process of reconstructing something. After that first deconstruction, did you settle on something?

**James:** I went into a blend of not knowing and not being confident in either direction. It was really a dichotomous frame — is young Earth creationism true, or is evolution true? Is it science or faith? I ended up trying to continue faith, not because it was logically obvious, but because I chose it as a basic orientation. It didn't quite work to rebuild the tower on top of choice, so it was a kind of superposition for a long while. But it freed me up to go out into the world and build a normal life at least.

I moved into AI in grad school — twenty-plus years ago, when AI looked nothing like today. It strikes me that if 25-year-old James was graduating now, he would totally have gone to work at an AI lab. But AI was going nowhere at that time, so I went into traditional computer software, building products. Building software was fun. And so I went into tech and did that for maybe fifteen years of a pretty traditional career.

**Shaun:** What was guiding you through that career?

**James:** Get married, have kids, succeed in career. One, two, three, bingo. And a sense of purpose in the work from creativity and service to people. You want to build a good thing, you want your customers to be happy. I was working in data visualization, and I really cared about helping people see and understand their data. That led me to becoming a process guy in the engineering department — figuring out better ways of working, trying to improve how the company functioned from the inside.

**Shaun:** I can see the echoes there — running clubs, looking at process, bringing people together. But eventually this falls apart for you. Walk us through that.

**James:** All the success, solving all the problems, ramping up my career through bigger and bigger challenges. It was the systems stuff, the figuring out change — after writing a ton of code and doing a couple layers of management, I stepped out into development practices for how we build software. I was finding that people are even more interesting and complicated than tech, and I was sort of falling from the tech stack into what I called the human stack below it. The human dynamics, the incentives, the psychology of people and teams and organizations — that became my playground.

I was full of purpose and meaning and having a great time. At one point we converted a 500-person engineering org to quarterly releases. Big change. Then it was 500 to 1,000 people, and that was really where I got stuck. Things stopped working in a real and tangible way, which was very surprising to me. I ended up having to give up a lot of my aims and ambitions, give up some of my goals, give up even my identity of who and how I thought I was.

**Shaun:** What was the message you were getting when everything logically seemed to be going well?

**James:** At first it wasn't the traditional "winning the rat race and it feels unfulfilling." It was literally just losing and getting smacked in the face. I keep setting my sights on bigger goals. We're gonna go for an even deeper transformation of a thousand-person org. And I just started to fail — fail hard.

Things that had worked for me and had been strengths — solve everything with my brain, figure out a plan, raise up the flag, charge forward — that started to not just not work, but actively backfire. Things that worked a few years before were now counterproductive in a larger context. And it was that whiplash of really getting smacked that got me to pause, to stop, to really reassess: what the heck is going on here, and what do I do now? And I didn't know.

**Shaun:** You finally reached your limit?

**James:** Finally reached my limit of complexity. Too big, too complex, too much. I couldn't solve it. That was very confronting — very challenging to the identity I'd built of me as the solver and the saver.

**Shaun:** Where do you go from there? I'm curious about the home life.

**James:** Honestly, it was the home life where it really hit me most. I was becoming super stressed, floundering and flailing, and I was bringing it home. My kids would have a fight with each other and I'd yell at them. It was starting to come out in my parenting — the frustration, the stress, the anger. And that really caught me: this is not who I want to be. Got me retooling in an even bigger way.

So that started a path of: I don't care what it takes, I have got to rework or tear apart as many layers as it takes to find some new path forward. Slamming myself against the brick wall harder and harder was not working. So that helped me tip into more of a giving up approach. Giving up on how I was attempting to do life.

Luckily I had a few trailheads. One was adult development psychology. The other was authentic relating — relational practices. Both of those I'd found in my pursuit of trying to figure out the human engineering of everything. They ended up being much more personal and much more like: I don't know who I am, who I want to be, how to be anything. But they were some tools that put some maps out there.

It was really the adult development maps and models that gave me a way to give up a little more fluidly. Maybe I could deconstruct all of this stuff one more time — deconstruct myself and my self-identity — and those maps said: oh, well, maybe there's something different. Maybe there's something more. Maybe there's something beyond the conventional American dream version of success and meaning and happiness. That ultimately led me out into a bigger, more free space.

**Shaun:** That reminds me that even though I was very scientifically minded, I always had things I was curious about that science couldn't explain — Carl Jung, supernatural stuff, ghosts and UFOs. Those came back to the foreground once the context changed.

**James:** Yeah, that resonates. And I've bumped into so many folks for whom that story is true — some sort of meaning crisis, some ceiling. To be able to go through what I call a developmental transformation — where the place from which you are standing and looking at the world, that place itself shifts and grows and becomes bigger — I've tasted that. It's really real.

**Shaun:** Can you explain a bit more about adult development and relational practices, and how those helped you build a new perspective?

**James:** There's a core to the phenomenon of noticing things that you didn't notice before — seeing things that didn't even appear to be things. They were just invisible in the environment, the water you were swimming in. And you can start to see that water.

You can go into a circling or an authentic relating group and start to see your own patterns just in the way you interact with other people. Something as simple as having a conversation — you can start to see the emotional layers, the self-analysis layers, the mental loops. There's this whole complex world in myself that I wasn't sensitive to before.

And seeing it in systems, groups, institutions — there's so much we don't think about. We just decide "that's just the way things are." The transformation from being lost inside something, not even seeing it, to noticing it — in that process, instead of being lost inside the thing, you become bigger. You become able to take a new perspective on it. You can see it from different angles. You can play with it. You can choose a new orientation to it.

I was still trying to figure out what this was in the early days of Intentional Society. And this mantra we have now — awareness, acceptance, integrity — that is exactly the phenomenon it points at. It was the subject-to-object shift and how to do it. The mental moves of noticing, welcoming, and reorienting. I was trying to figure out what had happened to me. So that was one motivation for starting Intentional Society — I knew this was amazing and I wanted to keep going and do it with friends.

**Shaun:** Bring it back to the end of your corporate career. What is that transition like, and how is this new way of noticing influencing how you manage through it?

**James:** I held on tightly enough and long enough that I actually physically lost — in the sense of getting reassigned out of the mega project I was on. I literally couldn't continue tilting that windmill the same way. And then I had a kind of developmental breakdown-breakthrough — they're kind of the same thing and go together. Coming out the other side really felt like a breakthrough.

Just the other day I remembered this movie — Office Space. The protagonist gets hypnotized and the hypnotist has a heart attack and he gets stuck in this hypnotized stage. He just starts floating through life, everything's great, there's no possible problems. There's a way in which I came back to work almost like that protagonist — everything's different, I feel different, the world is different, everything is new and fresh and fascinating.

My caring had totally reoriented. I was: okay, I'm still here at this company. I'm going to figure out what happened and figure out who I am now. How do I orient? I don't even know if I want to quit work or quit tech. Maybe I find a new way of being here. I actually accidentally started doing some internal corporate activism there for several months. But it was about a year later that it became clear on all sides — I've explored enough here. This feels done. I'm done with this. They're done with me too. And it felt complete.

**Shaun:** What then? You take a leap and quit?

**James:** Yeah. Take a leap. It was time. And even that was assisted by the fact that this was precisely the start of the pandemic, early 2020. That made for a real easy transition: yep, it's time, go home, help my kids with remote school.

I was very lucky at that point. I'd joined this startup thirteen-ish years prior. The company had grown 100X, gone public, gotten bought by Salesforce. So I was fortunate to be able to say: got enough money, done with that. And I quit. It felt like, yeah, I'm pretty sure I'm quitting the whole tech industry. I don't need a job, and if I don't need a job, I don't think I want another one. Done, done, done. I called it retirement-resignation-graduation. Wasn't sure exactly what it was. Felt like graduating from another phase of life.

Went home at the start of the pandemic and deliberately did nothing career-wise. It was going to take as long as it took. It took about six months to just sit with deconstructing my identity yet again: who am I now that I'm not a worker and I don't have a career? Who am I now?

That actually turned out to be a little easier than the last deconstruction, because I was finding more at the heart and center of things. Am I falling or flying? I don't know. But I'd taken the big leap. And so to just ask myself: who am I now, and what am I called to do? The question shifted from "what do I want to do" to something more like: what does the universe want from me? What is the right service and right orientation to myself, to others, and to the world?

It was less than a year of intentional, deliberate drifting. And I really started to get a sense of a pull or a call that felt different — not attached to a "therefore I will be the hero" kind of meaning and orientation. That had been my operating frame for the prior twenty years: I'm going to solve or save something, I'm going to be the hero, therefore I will have value, therefore I will be lovable. This felt different. Small and subtle in comparison for a while. But it was there, and it kept gently tugging on me.

It was more like following a dowsing rod — trying to sense where the hidden underground reservoir of water is. Blindly but intuitively following that sense of: okay, this one next step. It was no longer "I'm going to build a ladder to this outcome." More just one foot at a time. This one thing feels good to do. It felt good to write something down and post it on a webpage. It felt good to start a weekly call and invite a bunch of friends and acquaintances I'd spidered across the internet, looking at communities and groups, trying to find: where are my people?

**Shaun:** I'm contrasting your transition with the last episode, with Carol, where it was really an individual practice — meditation — that helped her tap into what she called the intuitive paradigm. Yours sounds similar but more relational. How did you learn to listen to that intuitive sense?

**James:** Yeah, there were individual practices. I'd gotten into meditation — the Sam Harris Waking Up app a few years prior. That is probably more typical. Most people's routes go through some kind of meditation retreat or psychospiritual awakening that comes through that vein.

It was pretty interesting for me that it snuck up on me through relational practices. That was powerful and transformational. It had more of a fundamental quality: this, of course, this happens in connection with people. This happens in the in-between us. More than sitting on my own pillow, isolated, meditating to awakening — which is there, which is wonderful. But it had more of that fundamental relationality flavor for me.

**Shaun:** Through what you've set up, I found that the relational is more powerful. I can spend all this time on my own, but then something pops up with someone else and it's just right there, very present, pushing you out of normal patterns.

**James:** Interaction is very catalyzing. Being in contact with other people — a spouse, partner, kids, family. What's the quote? "You think you're enlightened? Go home and spend Thanksgiving with your family." Other people are confronting and challenging. It's very catalyzing. And I found that very provocative and very obvious in the relational practices. It's all looped together — the inner, the inter, the exterior, they feed into each other. Using the relational as the entry point and catalyst for inner and exterior change seems to be a winning formula. And so that is very fundamental to Intentional Society.

**Shaun:** Do you have an example from that time of a relational practice that really impacted you?

**James:** It's still burned into me — something like the second circle I ever did, in a second-floor attic yoga studio in Fremont, Seattle. Sitting there, and somebody expressed irritation towards something I'd spoken in the circle. I interpreted it as directed at me, and I froze. Fight-flight-freeze — I really froze, probably for most of the rest of the session.

But it was in a practice environment. We were all there to practice together. So it was pretty obvious: oh, I have this reaction. Look at this reaction inside of me. If somebody was vaguely slightly upset in my direction — whoa, that was a big deal for me. It was threatening, but it was also there as this wow-cool factor. There's a whole new realm opening up in front of me that I was just too dense to see before. So it was exciting too.

**Shaun:** I can see how that could be useful, because the alternative could be ruminating on it, getting angry, getting stuck trying to explain it.

**James:** I probably thought about it for weeks afterwards. But yeah, it was precisely the: oh, well, I can see this now. I see that I'm doing this. I see that I've done this for thirty years, even back as a kid. I'd lay on my bed and ruminate, run over every negative thing that happened, or try to imagine every potential negative thing that could happen, in order to mentally prevent bad social outcomes. It was amazing to get perspective on that — and then actually unfold into: oh, well, I can just stop doing that. What a revelation.

**Shaun:** What a relief. So you're seeing the power of these practices, and you decide to start your own. We're still in pandemic times.

**James:** I was still like: wow, this is the best thing since sliced bread, what else is out there? The first six, nine months of this exploratory weekly Sunday thing — tentatively called Intentional Society — was a bit of a tour. A buffet. Try every dish at the buffet. A whole bunch of different relational practices, one after another. That was just a lot of fun and a lot of growth.

**Shaun:** But you end up finding things that become the foundation.

**James:** I made a slide at year one that was like: here are the nine core practices, three columns, real inner game, real interpersonal, bringing in the world. Figuring out the taxonomy of different practices. That settled down after a while. We started going back to our favorites, honing in on what is at the core of these — what are these mental motions, what are these psychological expansions we're experiencing? About two and a half years in, I think, we got the three-word mantra figured out.

**Shaun:** So we're getting close to present day. How has the expansion gone — from small exploratory community to what IS is now?

**James:** From my position as founder, it's been interesting to watch. I knew I wanted this thing to not be about me, and yet I was the one starting it and leading it. And maybe any initiative has that arc — how does it go from the source or founder, and how does it grow to become shared over time?

I always knew I wanted that. But in fact, that was still some of my shadow material in the early years. Having that egalitarian hangover — really didn't want to be the spotlight, really didn't want to be the owner or controller of the thing, really wanted it to be this communal consensus kind of place. And that has its own failure modes. Tyranny of structurelessness. So that was part of my growth arc.

I tried various things — create a stewardship circle, let's do sociocratic governance. Some of those things were just a little too early. I was trying to push them onto other people because I was uncomfortable leading. Push them on earlier than the natural flow of the group was calling for. So I've had to learn from some epicycles of that.

**Shaun:** I noticed some of that — you looking like here's opportunities for other people to lead and step up, and people going: well, James, you're the best at this, this is your thing. I think more stepping up now. It's one of those slow things you just have to let happen.

**James:** The key shift — maybe year four or five — is the most important leadership lesson I've learned. There's an inversion of what you think, if you're trying to share and distribute leadership.

My natural impulse was: I will step out of the center, I will lean back, in order to make space for other people to take more space. What I've learned instead is: if you're the leader in a space, people are already copying you. If you lean back, everybody else leans back too. There's a withdrawal energy that's contagious.

Instead, there's this paradoxical thing — lean in harder. Lean in fully yourself, but in a way that expands the space. Creates more room for other people to lean in too. You lean in together. And you have to drop the scarcity mindset — the sense that there's only one leader and so many executive slots. Expand the pie. Encourage the source energy of every person. We can all tap into that energy of whatever it is that we are passionate about, that calls to us: this is mine to do, this is mine to serve.

Creating an ecosystem and spaciousness for all of this is the path I hope we're on. And I've been finding more and more of the fluidity — in this space, I'm holding the container. In this other space, somebody else is holding it. In yet another space, another person. Finding that fluidity of contextual roles, the leadership, the source-ship — that feels amazing. I'm still learning and still growing in that. I hope we are learning together.

**Shaun:** I love that shift. Going from a hesitant leader — "who else can step up and do this, I do it all the time" — to more of an example, like you said.

**James:** I want to flag something that's still on my edge. There's a way in which if it's my space and I'm creating an opportunity for somebody else to lead something, we're already trapped in a frame of "you're doing this inside my space." It's not wrong, it's not bad — it's a mental construct. But ultimately it's not my space or your space. It just belongs to the universe, really. And we're all just stewards, figuring out how to dance in a way that avoids the my-space, your-space thing. This is really tough. There are still things I need to learn about how to dance more cooperatively.

**Shaun:** I'm thinking of the podcast itself as an example — it was my idea brought into your organization. I've had the ownership and freedom, and I can run it how I want. But then I want to check in: am I doing this in a way that makes sense for Intentional Society? Is there any feedback on how I'm doing this? Without giving up ownership. And then as I say ownership, I question it. I also want to hold this as something of its own — I'm just stewarding it, helping it find its way.

**James:** Yeah.

**Shaun:** In the process of doing that, I catch myself: oh, I am holding onto this as my own thing and part of my identity. I want to tell people I have a podcast. But how do I cling to that, and how do I be okay if it doesn't work, if it wants to change in some way? Can I make sure it still honors Intentional Society? All these things that come into play as you take on something and push yourself.

**James:** That is a great example. And I'm really happy with the way this has unfolded. This came from you. It wasn't "I had an idea, let's do an Intentional Society podcast, who wants to lead it?" I remember asking you: what do you want this podcast to be called? Do you want it to have the Intentional Society name on it? The thing that felt best to me was asking you: how do you want this to be held?

**Shaun:** Yeah. And then that reinforces my ownership of it. And if I can make the move to say "I have less ownership, it's not the Shaun Button podcast, it is the Intentional Society podcast" — okay, then it's my decision to keep it in there. And I like that decision because it keeps it less mine, more something I'm stewarding in the space.

**James:** For the record between you and I — I think both of us would probably say "I don't know" when asked about the future of this podcast. I'd say that. And you've got some hopes, some visions, some dreams. But between us, I think we have a state of: we'll see what this thing wants to be and how it wants to unfold. If your path diverges from the podcast, or if the podcast diverges from Intentional Society, we could discover that. Or we could keep on going together for longer and longer.

**Shaun:** Yeah. That we agree on that — I think that's the fundamental thing. Because if one of us was grasping on to some outcome or way of doing it, there would be conflict.

**James:** Yeah. And I could rattle off six different names of different groups in this space that have fallen apart — relational division or coordination problems ripped them apart. That's still very much an active learning across the whole field. We'll see how our alignment goes when tension or conflict arises. I'm glad to be here with you, Shaun, and glad to be involved in co-sourcing aspects of Intentional Society together.

**Shaun:** Yeah, it's a great relationship. I really cherish it. So with all this learning — what are the next moves? You've committed some money to this, you're expanding, supporting the wider community. How is that going and where is it going?

**James:** I can feel the fundamental I-don't-know-ness sitting just right underneath the surface. I don't know where this is going. It's going to go where it wants to go. In one sense I'm just along for the ride. In another sense, though, I am devoted. I am committed. I am in service to this thing — and I mean not even the thing named Intentional Society itself, but the bigger thing underneath it. The moon that all these fingers are trying to point to.

It's a life vow, that level of commitment. Because I can think of no other way that my being and my life and my orientation would be, than to ultimately be in service of all life. If there's anything I have faith in — it's a prediction, it's an observation of what already is true. How could I not continue on this same journey of unfolding, of growth, and of service throughout my life? So yeah, all of my energy, my time, my money, my relationships — it's kind of everything.

And I continuously check myself: is this the kind of religious fundamentalism I grew up with? But there's something that feels right about it in a way that the fundamentalism never did. There was always a tinge of coercion or collapse around it. That's just not here. There's this sense of fundamental goodness. Having faith that I have some ability to connect to that and tap into something that is true and faithful and trustable in guiding me in future actions — there's something there that I'd say I have faith in. Because it feels true.

I don't know where the world is headed — with AI, with geopolitics, with social and cultural evolution. But in another sense, yeah, I'm just going to keep riding with it and dancing with it and continue trying to make sense of it all. Acting in devotion, one footstep at a time.

**Shaun:** This is probably the last question. Let's see if you can do it — point at the moon. What is it that you're so devoted to?

**James:** What is it? How do we point at it?

It's love. And in particular a kind of love that — there's this betweenness. It's most powerful for me not in the zoomed-all-the-way-out version of "love the universe and the universe loves me," nor all the way zoomed in to self-love. It is those things, but there's a fundamental relationality to love that I feel in my access to it. That was maybe bred into me in terms of Christian theology, the Trinity — "let us make man in our image." The relationality of Father, Son, Holy Ghost. When I go back to that, something really connects: there is something fundamental here. Love is between two entities. And there is a beauty and a goodness in that truth that could not be otherwise.

Love feels relational. And maybe that is the best finger I have to point at the moon — discovering the richness and the complexity and the beauty of connection, of relationship, and of dancing the dance.

**Shaun:** Thanks. That's a great way to sum it up, James. It's all about love.

**James:** Ah, but it sounds so cliché.

**Shaun:** Yeah.

**James:** Every cliché is harboring an underlying truth. But yes — love is the answer.

**Shaun:** Thanks for being on. This has been great. And best of luck in the future of love.

**James:** Thanks, Shaun. This has been terrific. Cheers.
