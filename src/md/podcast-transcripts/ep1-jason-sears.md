# Episode 1 — Jason Sears: Slow Work, Urgent Purpose

**Guest:** Jason Sears, co-founder of VillageCo
**Host:** Shaun Button
**Recorded:** August 2026

---

**Shaun:** Hi Jason, thanks for joining today.

**Jason:** Happy to be here. Thanks.

**Shaun:** So, how are you arriving?

**Jason:** I'm in Boise, Idaho. It's August 2026 and we've had a very smoky, fire-filled summer. I'm grateful for the last few days — the skies have been a little clearer and some blue is coming back out. It's been a busy, fun summer. And I'm enjoying settling into this moment with you, talking about the broader context of the fire and the change we're going through as a society.

**Shaun:** All right. So before we get there, can you paint a picture of who Jason was before you arrived in this space?

**Jason:** Who I was. I have always been involved in nonprofits — I have my sister to thank for that. She was working with kids who had autism and got me involved with the Boys and Girls Clubs to help set up a computer lab. That was way back when, and ever since then I've worked with a number of different organizations. There's always been a part of me in touch with the sense that something's not quite right — that there are things we need to be actively doing to really support each other and build the kind of communities we want to have.

That orientation has always been there. The challenge and the rub has always been: where's the career in this? There's a constant tension of how do you get a good-paying job doing good? That's been twenty years of effort. For a long time, the answer was fundraising. I've helped a lot of organizations fundraise, and it did pay well toward the end of it — but things were shifting for me at that time too.

**Shaun:** Okay. Expand on that. Give us a better picture — how old were you? How were you working with these organizations? How did that feeling you mentioned evolve?

**Jason:** When I was in my late twenties, I was the CEO of Big Brothers Big Sisters, and I was really proud of the work we were doing with mentoring programs. And then one day I encountered a TED talk by Alain de Botton called "Atheism 2.0." For many reasons, it just resonated deeply. It immediately connected a lot of threads I'd been carrying for a long time.

One of those threads came from a mentor at the Boys and Girls Clubs who basically told me: all that matters are relationships. That's all it is. This whole building can burn down. The computer lab can crash. All of that can fail, and we could still have a meaningful moment with every one of these kids — because they're not here for the stuff. They're here for the relationships.

In my work over the years, across different organizations, I'd just seen that to be true. The impact we like to see happening is the result of nourishing deep relationships with each other and in community. So that was one thread.

Another thread, in that moment, was that my own passion was drifting. I felt like I was working downstream. A lot of the stuff I was helping with was treating symptoms of a problem, and I wanted to get to more of the root cause. So that TED talk really woke me up to the need for building a culture — the need to revisit our sense of what it means to be a part of a community.

The idea was that maybe churches were actually providing a great service and doing a lot of good work, and by abandoning them on the grounds of faith and science, we'd missed out on a lot of things that were nourishing us. Maybe we could lean into that model — what's working in those scenarios — to fix some of these upstream problems.

That was the rub: I think I want to work more upstream. So what does that mean? How do I get involved with an organization whose purpose is just to build relationships — to bring people together and nourish that social fabric?

**Shaun:** Do I have it right? The point of the TED talk was basically going back to the good things that religion did, without the dogma, in a new context?

**Jason:** Yeah, Alain talks about how most people go to church for the community — that's where you celebrate births and weddings, where you grieve together at deaths. You bring your problems there, you get support, you find others to relate with. There's also encouragement to travel, to go on a pilgrimage, to look for the deeper threads in life — something beyond just going to a job.

Church provided that for a lot of people. And he was commenting on the trend of the nones — the religiously unaffiliated — people who are becoming largely non-affiliated with formalized religion. There have been many experiments in trying to come up with what's the new church, the atheist church. Sunday Assembly is probably one of the bigger ones. There certainly is a wisdom of community that we're trying to touch, trying to get back in contact with.

**Shaun:** Yeah, I love that. My grandparents had all left church, so I was never really exposed to it, and I had that resistance to the religious dogma and ideas. But then I came to realize — I am missing something. There seems to be something there. Something like the Intentional Society Sunday meetings — I've heard multiple people say "this feels like church for me," because we're meeting as a group, talking about deep topics, and building those relationships.

**Jason:** Mm-hmm.

**Shaun:** So what's the next step? This sparks something for you. Do you leave Big Brothers Big Sisters? How do you set out from there?

**Jason:** It begins with side projects. For several jobs later, as I transitioned and moved to Seattle, had different opportunities, there was always this feeling — whatever I was doing didn't feel like *the* thing. Even though I was in nonprofits, even though I felt good about the work I was doing, it still felt like it wasn't what I was really called to.

So I started side projects, trying different ways of bringing people together — kind of like what Intentional Society is doing — poking at it with a very low degree of competency. I did not know what I was doing. I had a sense of it; I thought I knew every time I tried something. But it just wouldn't work out. I started meetup groups with various names — Thrive Project, Justice League, all kinds of stuff.

What became the great tension was that my energy at work just wasn't there. I just didn't want to be doing it. The only reason I was there was because I needed to make a living.

Once COVID hit, that's where major changes happened. I had a group that was meeting in person monthly, and so that went virtual when COVID happened. We encountered a bunch of new people — everyone was trapped and looking for something. That's where I encountered Open Space Technology, a facilitation technique, and it really was the thing I had been looking for in terms of how to empower self-organizing when you bring people together. That was the first glimmer: okay, this is actually closer to what I was trying to do.

**Shaun:** Can you expand on Open Space — what it is and how it filled that gap? Were you finding some of these things loosely, and this put it all in a package that made it easier to understand and employ?

**Jason:** Yeah, it has to do with the energy that goes into bringing people together. Prior to that, I was facilitating — coming up with topics, relational practices, things we could do together when we gathered. It was a big effort, and getting other people to co-hold it with me was always a challenge. People are happy to attend something, but to actually co-lead it requires a lot of time and unique people at the right moment in their lives. That wasn't happening, and it wasn't sustainable. I could get something going for a few months, maybe a year, and then I couldn't sustain it.

Open Space is a very simple idea. There's a history to it worth exploring, but just the practice: you have a group of people, usually with a theme or some shared question we're pondering together. As a group, you come up with a list of specific topics to dive into related to that theme — we call it the marketplace. Then you break into smaller groups. Everyone chooses which group they want to be in. If you want to move around, that's totally fine. You have a longer period of time in a small group to discuss the topic, then you come back together as a larger group.

The reason it hit me as an aha moment was: there's no preparation required. People just show up with what's alive in their bodies and their minds. When questions get asked, there are always people who say "I want to talk about this" or "this happened to me and I want to hear what you think." It provided the gathering experience as something anyone can contribute to. And as a practice that happens regularly, it becomes something you can rely on. You can even have themes that connect between gatherings.

It made facilitating way easier and accessible. Now others can lead it; it doesn't require a huge level of skill, and the group holds it together. I realized that with regular gatherings using Open Space, different people can shine each time. That's exactly what I was hoping for — what I had been looking for without being able to name it.

**Shaun:** Okay. So we're in COVID now, you're running these meetings with the new format. What starts to happen? How does that build on what you're trying to do?

**Jason:** We ran experiments. We had a Village Seattle that grew quite a bit, meeting weekly virtually. Then we started more experiments. I was working at Northwest Center at the time, a pretty large nonprofit, and I thought we could do a workplace village — growing that culture within a company, nourishing connections between people. We started that, and it was great. We did a family village — a lightly structured family reunion on a Zoom call. I moved to Boise and started one here. We started one with a nonprofit called TARP. We ran about ten different experiments with villages, seeing what that experience was like for members.

What I heard was a lot of support. People loved it. It was a great format. And not just more or less than other things — people love coming together in general. What was *different* about these experiments was that the effort in organizing them was very minimal. That was a key ingredient to what I wanted to see happen: can communities organize themselves so they have a space to meet each other and coordinate? I was seeing evidence that this is possible. People can do this with very little effort.

By 2022, we thought: let's get organized. This was the off-the-cliff moment for me. I had a paying job. I was focused most of my time there, really feeling stressed trying to get this going, feeling like this was really alive. So it was: I'm ready to jump. I want to switch over — but this isn't a career. I'm not going to be making a salary.

I thought I could fundraise for it. I do a lot of fundraising, so I was pretty comfortable going for it. And it was way more difficult than I was expecting. I got support, but not at the level I was hoping for. Pretty soon I had already committed to leaving my job, my savings was dwindling, the income wasn't quite there. I was approaching that cliff moment: should I jump back into the career world? Or just see what happens on the other side?

Luckily I had some friends I could talk to. They helped me out, and the way forward was so beautiful and so unexpected for me.

And it was crazy at the time. The idea was to live simply — if I need less, I don't need to raise as much. I didn't have a family; I'd gone through a divorce. So it ended up being this perfect place in my life where I had the ability, the privilege, to live more minimally. And it allowed me to focus a full-time amount of effort into a project.

So I leaned into receiving public support. And because my needs were so minimal, the fundraising I had done could support other people too. We now have several volunteers who all receive what we call gifts — essentially stipends for people who can commit a certain number of hours a week to the organization.

That shift let me leave my job, have lots of time, balance my life. Living minimally definitely took some adjustment. But it was the thing that let me get out of the old system. Now I feel unencumbered and quite liberated. I can focus all of my time — this is all I do. I just bring people together.

So I went off the cliff, and magic — wings appeared. I'm really happy that happened.

**Shaun:** That's funny. I've heard almost the exact same thing before about that scary cliff.

**Shaun:** And it's great to see your perspective now, where you're happy about it, even though it must have been scary at the time.

**Jason:** At the time, yeah.

**Shaun:** Was there anything specific that somebody said to you that really stood out — that reassured you and helped you make that big move?

**Jason:** It wasn't any one person. I'm open with my community and friends about how I'm doing, and in those conversations I'm always hearing support: this is good, this is going in the right direction, this is worthy. A few people have offered pretty significant gifts that have been very inspirational.

But it's so crazy because I have tried to give up several times since then. There are just these times — a few key volunteers leave, something doesn't go well in one of the community groups, things don't work out, and it seems like a downtrend. And I'm like, I just need to get a real job.

So I have these moments where I let that be a possibility. Maybe it's time for this to go. I can look for something — that's definitely something I can do. Earlier this year I was doing this, found a job listing, started going down that road. Not far beyond that, all the signals were pointing: this is not for you.

Then there was a particular village gathering a few months ago. At the end, everyone shared — how are you, what are you taking out of this experience together? It was such a rich share. Some folks new, some who'd been there a while, some who'd opened up and shared things. Just hearing the diversity of what was being shared in that moment was a huge signal — a deep signal of: yeah, this is important and worthy of effort.

And then I could get back to: ups and downs are going to come, that's just part of it. So I re-chose. I re-chose to be where I'm at. I re-chose to enter into the work.

There's no one thing that does it, but there are definitely big moments with lots of thoughts and conversations that add up. And I return back to this whole, rich feeling of being on a journey, on a path of some sort. I've tried to let go of the knowingness of it. My path is not a strategic plan. Even VillageCo as an organization is not necessarily my path — it's just something I'm tending right now because that's what I feel I'm supposed to be doing. I let go of knowingness, and I return back to where I'm at. It's been good. I'm really grateful for the whole journey.

**Shaun:** Yeah, that's a great perspective. I'm wondering — what does that feeling of almost going back to the real world, back to work, feel like to you?

**Jason:** The first thing I think about is going out to eat — that's the thing I miss most. I would spend a lot of my money on food, and I have a very different relationship with food now. Oh man, being able to go out to eat whenever I want, once or twice a week — all the restaurants, all the good dining experiences. I get really excited about that.

Vacations. Traveling is not something I do much now. I do have a perspective that I can travel the world in just my little region right here — I've got waterfalls, I've got mountains. I'm very lucky to travel very cheaply here. But I used to do cruises. I loved being on the ocean. I lived in Africa for two years in the Peace Corps, and being in another culture was a transformative experience I'd like to have more of.

So all the benefits — yeah, that would be so cool. But then there's this awareness that I know what's going to happen. I'm not going to let go of any of the stuff I'm doing. I'm going to keep doing it on the side with whatever spare energy I've got, and I'm going to start stretching myself thin again. I'm going to try to do too much.

I've been there, done that, and I know it doesn't feel right. I really enjoy my flexibility right now. Every day I go on nice long walks. Right now I'm in a production at our local community theater — that would be really hard to do if I was working full time. I'm just able to do a lot more, and it feels right to me.

**Shaun:** Yeah. It's funny that you went to all the benefits of having more money. I was expecting "it would feel horrible" — but no, it's: I can see how I could go back and what it would be like.

**Jason:** Yeah.

**Shaun:** And it's not so horrible, but I guess that probably helps with the uncertainty. Even if you do go back and do things differently, there are benefits to that, and you'll manage.

**Jason:** Yeah, but like you said — there's not a dread, but there is an awareness that it wouldn't be good for my mental health. You never know what kind of boss you're going to get, never know what kind of company culture you're walking into. There are so many unknowns when you join an organization, and you're really committed to being there. It comes with a lot of pressure. So yeah, all that is there as well. It's a system, for sure.

**Shaun:** Okay, so let's try and get back to the timeline. You're into VillageCo, you're starting that. How does it evolve? Are there more influences on how you format it, more people who contribute to it? How does it get from the start to what it is now?

**Jason:** Yeah, one of the great challenges is co-creating — really co-creating. It's easy to co-create when there's a money pot around you, when you're all making something together. So there's a lot of co-creation that happens in businesses and the for-profit world. But the real challenge is co-creating when no one's getting paid and there's no direct benefit.

I use the metaphor of carrying water — from my time in Africa, where if you don't have a well or running water, every day someone has to go get it. It's a job almost anybody can do, nobody wants to do it, but it contributes so substantially to the health of everybody in the household. There's a lot of water carrying in co-creating, especially when it comes to tending all the little things that need to happen in an organization.

So it starts with me doing most of the work. The groups we were making decisions in felt like rubber stamp committees. I'd come in like, "here's what I think," and everyone would say, "well, you put all the time in, so yeah, go for it." There was this deep intention — still exists today — of how do we get more legitimate co-creation happening?

That's where Sociocracy came in. Another beautiful gift from the universe. As soon as I saw it, I thought: thank you, I knew there had to be something.

The idea of Sociocracy is that we make decisions by consent — we decide together. There are so many assumptions when you first encounter that which need to be worked through. It's a whole different way of relating and working together. We've been practicing it for three years now. We have about twelve circles with around thirty volunteers — a few at the center who are in many circles, and others who are in just one, contributing in different ways. But everyone has the opportunity to jump in as much or as little as they want.

To me, the way in which we build things is as important as the things we're building. We're not only creating a new kind of civic club — that's what VillageCo has evolved into as a strategy — we're doing it by genuinely co-creating it, with an organization structured around consent-based decision making. It slows everything down in a very good way.

There's a quote I love: *Slow relational work with an urgent purpose.* We carry the urgency and the slowness at the same time.

**Shaun:** And so that solved the problem of you coming up with everything? Does it bring people in to create more on their own?

**Jason:** Yeah, "solve" is a strong word I don't get to use that much in this work. And I would say yes. I had to relearn a lot myself, because as a leader I'm used to people relying on me for ideas and direction. So I would come into the circles with these ideas, and as we got good at practicing Sociocracy, they would be rejected.

I'd sit there like — you're going to reject my idea? I'm the one doing this work. But I'd find my way to: okay, what do I do with this? So I had these really powerful moments of big ideas and projects being rejected, and then learning how to do it the right way, sociocratically.

In Sociocracy, you start with the issue statement: do we all agree this is an issue? Do we all see the same thing here? That's often why people don't contribute in a meeting — they don't get the issue being talked about. So you first align on the problem. Then you build a proposal together — it's not my proposal versus yours. Your idea and my idea are both part of this proposal. Both can be in there even if they're very different. There's a process for tuning that into a final proposal that everyone made together.

I still do about 80% of the work, but it genuinely is co-creative work, and it feels good because I'm putting effort into stewarding other people's ideas and making sure everyone feels represented in what we're creating.

And the group's ideas are 100% better than my ideas. I can't count how many times I've thought "this is so good, I'm so excited" — and it falls flat. We come up with something else, and I realize I missed 80% of it. I didn't even think about that part, or what that impact would have been. The process also surfaces my biases and my habitual patterns. I've grown a lot through it. I'm a much better collaborator because of this practice. We all are. I'm really proud of what we've built collectively.

**Shaun:** Yeah. I've seen how people can say "this is my idea" and dig in, defend it, go with it, and not want to hear anything else.

**Jason:** Yeah.

**Shaun:** So it sounds like a slower process, like you pointed to.

**Jason:** Yeah. It takes a little more effort, but I can see how everybody feels like they're part of the way forward when it's been this incremental building on ideas with everyone contributing.

Yeah. There's this — go at the speed of trust. If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together. If you want to do something big, you better be going slow. The action of that slowness is alignment. We all come in with such a rich diversity of experiences and perspectives, and we're just not aligned. Aligning around an issue takes so much time. But once you're aligned, now you can go fast. You have this common shared body of language around this proposal, you know what you're talking about, and things can move a lot quicker. There's a patience to getting there.

**Shaun:** Right, yeah. It seems like an inversion — usually the impulse is to skip to solving the problem. It's like, no — first figure out what the problem is.

**Jason:** Yeah, exactly. I think we all have experiences from traditional work environments where the boss comes in and boom, here's the thing. And we're paid to do that thing. It's hard to get out of that old model. I think we have to acknowledge that doing things together is a much higher level of complexity, and that makes it fragile. There are setbacks. That's part of it too — just being in it for the right reasons. Which is why Sociocracy starts with: we're all deciding together. That creates the shared intention that gets you through the highs and lows.

**Shaun:** Yeah, and that points back to what you said about relationships. This seems to keep the relationships more intact and focused on how we're relating around the problem, rather than just on a result and getting things done. So how has that played out in the organization?

**Jason:** Yeah. I haven't compared this to other experiences, so I don't know about "better" in some objective sense. There is research on Sociocracy that makes the case for better outcomes through doing things together. But where we're at — in this rich local community space, really intentional community space — the benefit we get is we're in contact with a lot of other things happening. We're not holding what we're doing too seriously. There's a lot of exploration and experiments. People are part of other things going on, and we learn through contact with other organizations.

The relationality is definitely a benefit. It's not for everybody — some people prefer their work to be separate from their personal lives, with more of a boundary. But I've had many people share that they really enjoyed the experience once they got through that transition.

We start every meeting with a check-in: how are you doing, how are you coming in? We end every meeting with a checkout: how did it go, what could have been better? Those give everyone a chance to just be human — to share both about their personal lives and about how we're working together. That feedback is really important.

We have the same kind of turnover that every organization has. People come and go for their own reasons — life happens, emergencies, moves, new jobs, spouses, kids. But we've had a good core number, and I think most healthy, growing communities will have that same story.

In terms of output, it's hard to say because we're trying something really new and different. We're trying to start a new kind of civic club. An example is Rotary — a lot of civic clubs struggle to grow. Rotary, the most successful international civic club, grows at about three to four percent a year when there's engagement. And that's with an established organization with a very clear way of going about things. We're still figuring it out. We've learned from them. We have a sense of what a flow could look like. But we've had a hard time getting new villages going. It takes years. We're in about ten villages with about two hundred members, and every village membership is churning. We just started a renewal period where everyone renews their memberships so we know who really wants to stay in this.

We're learning. We're growing. We're shrinking. And it's all part of it.

**Shaun:** Right. Can you expand on the current approach and what you're trying to do? You've got all these different groups — what's the intention with them, how do they work?

**Jason:** Every civic club has a meeting pace. That's really fundamental to our approach — there's a regular meeting. You mentioned the Sunday IS calls. It's so critical to have that pattern: I can show up at this time, at this place. Getting that happening in neighborhoods is really the objective.

We've constructed an agenda over time through all these experiments, drawing on a lot of facilitation techniques. We've really focused on light structure — that's critical, especially for diverse communities. We're all coming in with different expectations. For some, too much rigidity is a turnoff. For others, if it's too loosey-goosey, they won't show up. We're trying to find that balance. There's a sense that structure sets us free, and we're trying to find the right structure.

We have a 90-minute program. It involves Open Space, some relational practices like one-on-ones. The key is loosely defining each component in a way where different members of the community lead them every time. That creates diversity in what you experience at every gathering. And then there are roles — just like any civic club has roles. Someone opens the door, someone books the venue. You have to be clear about what the roles are so it's easy for people to pick them up.

There are four components to the program: the grounding, the acknowledgement, the community connections (the one-on-ones), and the Open Space. The grounding is a moment of reflection — some people lead it as a meditation or some breath, some with a poetry reading or some music. It can be done any number of ways, and it's so great when people do something different with it. The acknowledgement is three to five minutes on your soapbox: tell us what's live for you, what you're passionate about. We all hop up over time and share, and the acknowledgement helps frame the gathering. Then we move into the connections and the Open Space.

That's what was developed in the first few years. Now we're asking: what does it actually take to start a new village? People with experience in the program, who've taken on the roles — those tend to be the natural folks to start something new. The challenge is that we're not starting an interest club or a hobby group. We're starting something very open and generic: just get to know each other. Marketing that, selling that, has been a huge challenge.

One thing we've learned is that you don't lead with the club. You don't walk around saying "I'm starting a club, who wants to join?" That doesn't work. What does work: I love pancakes. I just hosted a pancake breakfast for my building last week. People show up for pancakes. I meet some people, chat with them, get to know who might be into doing this kind of thing, get their number, we organize the next pancake thing.

The deep lesson is that these clubs are for places that already have relationships. You have to discover or shine a light on the existing network of relationships and what's already alive. That's where it starts from.

**Shaun:** Right — like you need something to gather around. You didn't just say "let's gather." You need something, like kids and a ball. You throw the ball out and they all gather around and start playing.

**Jason:** Yeah. And what we've learned with existing villages is that the reason people will visit, the reason people come and check it out, is because they were invited by someone who's already a member. That's the number one reason. The invitation matters, but not as much as the person giving it.

That's why we have to start with something else just to get people to know each other. Find people who feel aligned. They can go to the website, we can talk about it like you and I are. And they're like, "yeah, we need to get together just to get to know each other — I want to know my neighbors. We're living in a time where knowing your neighbors really matters to me. I want to be able to trade food if we need to. I want to be able to help." Those are the people who will actually help start a village, because they'll get the deeper part of it. And then they'll invite people individually — that's why people will show up.

So yeah, we've started a couple of programs. We have an annual cohort program — time-bounding things is helpful for people to direct some energy into something. We have a Village Core program — a regular twice-monthly meeting where leads of villages come in and share where they're at and get support from each other. And then we have Village Nomad, our online village, which is where we all take turns hosting, grounding, acknowledging, and even stewarding Village Nomad itself — to get comfortable and familiar with organizing community groups before you do it in your local community.

It's all working. It's growing. I'm very excited about it. Every time we do something, it gets a little better, a little more refined, a little more simple and direct. I have no idea where we're going or how long it'll take to get there, but I'm enjoying the ride.

**Shaun:** And how are you doing through all this? You're taking on a lot of the work still, excited about it, it seems to be growing — but what's the feeling now, compared to where you were when we started?

**Jason:** There's definitely more people helping with the work, so that feels good. And I've learned that in times past when I had that feeling of "I'm doing all the work, no one else cares as much" — the real problem was I didn't know how to support others in helping me. I didn't know how to help others help me. I know how to do that now, and it's slow, but it works.

An example: bookkeeping. I have a background in it, so I just did it all myself. Getting someone else to help with bookkeeping is no small task — they need access to accounts, they need to know QuickBooks, we need an agreed procedure for where we're storing things and how we're organizing information. It took years. But I documented my process, chunked it off into little pieces, had people help with certain chunks, and they gradually learned more. Now, almost four years in, I have one volunteer who can do 100% of the bookkeeping. There's redundancy, which feels really good. I can see the pathway to even more people being able to contribute.

So yeah, there's definitely a sense of more contribution. I've learned a ton about collaboration. But the biggest benefit I've gotten from this whole experience is just being more present in my life — more present in life itself. An appreciation for life. I can't take this too seriously because it just doesn't go fast enough for that. I just have to wait for the next meeting. So what am I going to do instead? I'm going to go enjoy that beautiful park near my building. I get out more. I spend time with family — I have a cousin with four kids I love spending time with. Just feeling more connected to the things that provide real, deep meaning.

**Shaun:** Yeah, I feel like I'm learning the same lesson — to allow things to go at their own pace. As I get more involved with Intentional Society, we get ideas for projects. How many months ago was it that I mentioned the podcast idea? I get the idea, I want to do it right away, there's a lot of energy there. But there's something about how it has its own pace, and you've got to go with that — even if you feel like you want to go faster.

**Jason:** That's great. Yeah.
