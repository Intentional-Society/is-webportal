# Episode 4 — Faith: Nothing Has to Be Thrown Away

**Guest:** Faith, founder of Soul School
**Host:** Shaun Button
**Recorded:** 2026

---

*Faith once had it all on the good track — a high-flying career in San Francisco tech startups. But in 2016, she walked away into a long, dark night. Today, she describes her life as blissful — not in an escapist way. For Faith, joy is what happens when all the emotions are allowed to be there. She talks about not feeling alone, even when she's lonely, about treating even her most painful experiences as something to cherish, and about life as a composition of dark and light, where nothing has to be thrown away. We get into her soul work, learning to trust what she calls soul logic, and why she thinks beauty is a better guide than right and wrong. Here's Faith.*

---

**Shaun:** Great to see you, Faith. Thanks for joining. How are you feeling today?

**Faith:** I'm feeling good. It's a nice day out. I just came back from a sit.

**Shaun:** A sit — what does that mean?

**Faith:** I tend to meditate in the morning and I tend to do it outside. I live in San Francisco, so it's possible. Looking at the water. This morning it was simply sitting — a Keith Damman kind of practice. Anyway, I don't need to go into it, but it's a type of meditation.

**Shaun:** We want to get a picture of Faith before you are who you are now. Wherever you think is an appropriate place to start, take us back.

**Faith:** That's so funny, because I sit with "Who am I?" in front of me to remind myself, otherwise I would forget. The question I thought you were going to ask me is: how did I get to the liminal space? Can I start with that?

I think I was one of those people who responded to the question of the meaning crisis — it was put forth by John Vervaeke, roughly in the pandemic era. That's how I touched into what we generally call liminal space. What was going on for me then — there are probably two tracks to my story. The dark track is that I was going through an extended dark night that really began in 2016. That was when I left my career track.

I was on what I'd call, kind of cheekily, the good track — the track of outstandingness in what society values. High performance, productive member. It was in tech startups. I loved it. But the dark track involves something completely not that. It goes to the other extreme. If reality is a bell curve, I've got the tail ends — really up there and then really taboo. And I was going through serious mental illness at the time. I could not afford professional care, and I was very alone. I'd jumped out of the matrix with no matrix in place. And when I happened upon the folks in the liminal space — part of that was the relational island that James was heading up at Intentional Society. It was one of the spaces where I found somewhere to be without needing to be put together.

**Shaun:** What was the feeling you had during that darkness, as you're still trying to follow the traditional path? Before 2016, still working but with this unease?

**Faith:** Unease — that's the best way to put it. Actually, the root of it goes back further than that. As a child, just looking around and seeing what the world is doing and what is supposed to be great, and getting this feeling like something ain't right. A little alienated. Like: for real? Is this what we're supposed to do?

But there was this tension with the conditioning that a good person does their best to be of service to society. The sense that I need to fit in, putting to the side the feeling that something is not right. I don't feel the same thing as everybody feels. So you start to think there must be something wrong with me. And then 2016 was just the last rise and fall of several in my career and life. The feeling was one of extreme discouragement — the will to live being quite not there.

There's also another angle, which is a question that struck me even as a young preteen: what is real? What is the nature of reality and how do I participate in it? I realized early on that it's really all about perception. The way experience unfolds. That's not different from how I feel today — I just know it in a very different way now.

**Shaun:** Did you have a framework to investigate that when you were younger? For me it was science — what is the nature of reality, let's look at science, I'll learn all these facts to understand the world.

**Faith:** I got all the frameworks from schooling. I'm a nerd, so I take those in and I love them. But with regards to myself, I'm an experience type — let me see for myself. None of the frameworks held me. Everything was provisional right from the start. I actually started my life as a child deeply religious — Christian, love-based, very much love everyone and be honest as first principles. That's baked into me from then.

Going back to relationality: that was something I didn't have a lot of development in — the middle of the bell curve of really meaningful relationships with people. What I wanted to experience was that middle. My life isn't conventional, so I can't have the normal kind of experiences that people have with kids and normal marriages and normal job histories. And I wanted to experience that — versus the extremes. So coming in was trying to get out of relational poverty and trying to rebuild with people who would be nourishing. That's why I'm part of Intentional Society and have gratitude for it.

**Shaun:** So the isolation, feeling different from other people, the need for more experiential confirmation of the world — is there anything else there that you think is contributing to that crisis, that dark night?

**Faith:** I just need a reason to live, maybe. Is that enough?

**Shaun:** Yeah. You need some meaning.

**Faith:** What am I going to do with the rest of my life? Despair. Repeated tries and ending up in the same place. Huge highs. I've been diagnosed bipolar since my twenties. There's quite some volatility — bipolar depression is not feeling bad, it's hospitalization, it's sitting on my couch for two years unable to move. It's an altered state. I don't want to go too dark on that — bipolar is just a collection of symptoms they choose to group as one thing.

**Shaun:** You're riding these waves of ups and downs. Still back in 2016, around then — how do you come into the things that start to show you a way out?

**Faith:** There's nothing more meaningful or important to me than the conscious transformation of my expression in this world. An inquiry into what is the essence of my design. So that's why it meant a lot to take a chance on a place where the things I thought were not acceptable to others — could I show who I am? Is there a place for that?

**Shaun:** Do you have an example of something you wanted that you felt wasn't acceptable?

**Faith:** I wanted to be fully okay with my experiences in life. I don't want to be pathologized and relegated to someone whose chances of a fulfilling life are slim. Because statistically, once you've had this many incidents of whatever, it's unlikely you'll ever work again or be okay or happy.

What I really want is to be able to express how positive I am about so many things, and how much love I have to give. I thought the workplace was where I'd find that — it was my family, my community. When I left it in 2016, it was chaos. The way companies end engagements — it's your family until it's not. And it's horrible.

**Shaun:** How did you come to the decision to leave?

**Faith:** It was a very traumatic ending — my last job. I was at the pinnacle of my career. I wasn't raised with psychology or healthy ego concepts. Feel your feelings? Like, what? So my history is: throw it away, never happened, I'm going somewhere else. I went on a spiritual journey. At the time it was really spurred by the discovery of some non-ordinary states that showed me the universe inside. I'm like: that's really real, and I don't care about whatever y'all are doing. I'm going to go there. What's really real?

**Shaun:** So leave and go on this adventure. I had that too — I never left my job because I had a family, so I tried to do them both. But I remember the moment: there's a call to adventure and I'm going to answer it. I'm scared to dive in, but that's where this unease is. What is it like when you start going into it?

**Faith:** I make really black and white decisions sometimes. Part of what I'm learning today is being more fluid about that. It was just a very charged decision — anger, hurt, and then this alluring mystery of: well, what else is there to reality? I need to find out for myself.

**Shaun:** As you step into that — what is it like? How do you go into that alternate reality?

**Faith:** I've always had easy access to that. My root in actual spiritual path work is in non-dual Tantric Shaivism, the Indic tradition. A lot of the experiences I had in that tradition were just like — it connected me with a sense of power that I didn't know how to hold. And it led to some of that serious mental illness. I think awakening can be quite dangerous if you don't have a strong ego foundation to come from. And I didn't. There was a lot of trauma — unintegrated pain, fragments of me.

The experience was of amazing energy mastery, tremendous power — and then sort of a loss of ambition to do anything having to do with making a life here. That's pretty archetypal, actually. You hit that, and then to make the turnaround and come back is key. And that's what I'm doing. But it can be quite scary if you don't have support.

**Shaun:** To tie it back to the timeline — is this pushing you to leave your job, or did you leave and then dive into this practice?

**Faith:** There's an overlap of about one year. I'd had that experience and told my teacher: I don't feel called to this anymore. But there was so much already entangling me into taking this new position. I couldn't get out of it without disappointing everybody. So go into it. Do the best I can. And I did, and it went really, really well until a clusterfuck of things at the end that I don't want to go into. But I was living with this whoa-big-realization mixed in with everything else for a year, and then I left.

**Shaun:** It seems like it was inevitable that it would fall apart. If you'd had this awakening that made the career feel unimportant — do you think it was going to happen regardless because of the change in you?

**Faith:** That's a good question. I believe there are reasons why things come to extreme crises that have to do with not dealing with something for a long time. So yes, that was the case. But let me be clear — that job, I was passionate about. I put my heart into it, into my teams, into the wellbeing of the company. That's what I commit to: love. It meant something to me personally, not just professionally. That's why it was so hurtful.

**Shaun:** I'm making up a story here — tell me if it's accurate — that this was a test. Can I get full meaning and love out of a job? My experience is no, it's just work. So you needed to find out that you can't find what you're looking for in this role.

**Faith:** I don't believe that. What I needed to find out was more along the lines of wisdom — to discern where there are messy boundaries. I'm not the only one. People get invested in workplaces, they get their needs met. It was love, yes, but it's a very blurry thing when you have a system that separates work and personal. I've never had a job where I was just a cog in the wheel. I started in the dot-com boom, where there was no one who could tell me what to do. We were the pioneers. That's really been the shape of my career.

And so that's why I'm part of Intentional Ventures — because I don't think it needs to be that way. One of the things that needs to be solved is goodbyes. I can't tell you how much it hurt to have to fire people when I didn't want to. I've sacrificed myself for that in the past — refusing to fire someone and getting fired myself. Because I'm extreme sometimes.

**Shaun:** When you do leave — what's getting you through? What's your reorientation?

**Faith:** Nothing, for some time. I did try to look for another job, but something died in me. I can't do what I don't feel passion for. So nothing was guiding me except: I'm going to go traveling. There was a lot of coping that didn't quite work. Went back to using drugs. Had some mental illness issues again. But I started to come out of it again around 2020. Everybody was indoors and freaking out, and I'm like: I've been living this forever. My life is uncertain as fuck. And I'm actually excited. Crisis is exciting because we get to start over.

I think 2023 was the real changing point. I'm a yogi, by the way — the evidence is there, whether I like it or not. I've been doing yoga philosophy and yoga practices privately for a long time. Once I got my mental illness somewhat under control medication-wise, I was able to read and write again.

I entered a purpose discovery program run by Jonathan Gustin at the Purpose Guides Institute — around finding your soul purpose. I have nuances around that, but at the time I was: if not now, when? So I took it. It changed me enough to reorient myself around soul work and what that entails. I've been doing that ever since. It's changed how I live, how I make decisions, how I perceive, how I feel.

**Shaun:** How did that work? Can you give examples of the practices or ideas that impacted you?

**Faith:** I think it's more a perspective — a perspective on what the human endeavor is. It takes spiritual intelligence, which is what I want to bring to the world. More spiritual intelligence into our systems, in terms of value and meaningfulness.

And there are many dimensions to soul work. Imaginal practices, which have a lot to do with the aesthetic. The liminal realm — the imaginal world as an ontological thing. I do a lot of psychopomp work, actually. Shamanic psychopomp work. Work with people who are grieving, facing death, or who've just lost somebody. I work with the Living Dying project here in Marin County. So yeah, liminality is something that soul work has a lot to do with. This dark night thing — where you're between life stages, there's a crisis of understanding who you are, and you need to go deeper into: who am I really?

**Shaun:** I'm very curious about that transition. You've arrived at a whole new perspective now. How did that happen?

**Faith:** It was making the decision to do this program and then really doing it. Making that commitment to try it for a year.

We all go around with a model of reality in our heads that has certain foundational assumptions — things you have to take as true, that can't be proven. If you flip one of those, you have to redo everything. For me, one of those things was soul. It's something everybody intuitively sort of understands — what a beautiful soul. But what if it were really a thing that you tended, in all its depth? Made contact with? Got into a relational partnership with, based on the utmost devotional commitment. What if souls continue? What if there is rebirth? Adopt an idea and see: how does that change your view?

**Shaun:** Yeah, for me it was: how do you let that lead? Because it was always rationality and looking to the external, playing a game. That's how I made decisions. And then there was a shift to: can I let this — in your words, soul — lead instead? Even when it doesn't make sense.

**Faith:** Right. And rationality, to me, is very much associated with an ought. You ought to be reasonable, you ought to be rational. That's in conflict with my intuitive side. In a job, I'm super rational — I can be counted on to make rational decisions. With regards to me and my life, zero. It's not unusual for me to go with what they call aliveness. And what I've come to is really learning soul logic — the language of soul. It calls more on intuition and a familiarity that goes beyond the cognitive mind. The cognitive mind is there and it's useful as hell, but there's more. And so yes, it does take trust. That's why sometimes you have to have your back to the wall and surrender. That's why crisis is often associated with it. But learning soul logic, how to hear, the sensitivity — that is kind of what I help people with.

**Shaun:** Right. But you learned how to do that yourself first.

**Faith:** You have to. The idea was probably planted from many inputs — the idea that there is a soul, that you might try imaginal methods. These things hit you sometimes with a resonance that feels like a knowing, a recognition: yeah, that's true. For me, that's true. I would never say for everyone. It's like what they say about transmission — something hits you and you're quieted, everything quiets. You're alert, you're steady, you're captivated. At least from a spiritual path perspective, that's what I go by.

And then it takes some effort — like any language you're learning. You have to do it, practice it, before you get fluent. Plus there are gifts that everyone's given to help them when they're not fluent yet. That's how I navigate today — counter-rational, I would say. Not counterintuitive. Counter-rational.

**Shaun:** My experience with that is that almost every time I have that type of insight, there's resistance. How do you deal with resistance?

**Faith:** Yeah, that's the work. That's what people don't like. That's what they call shadow work. All inner work is shadow work, isn't it? Making conscious what's not conscious.

It takes time. You have to separate yourself from what you see is happening — the witness kind of thing. Step back from any one thing you think is you. You go through a process of discovering, testing, discovering: what you thought was absolutely necessary, what you thought "there's no way out of this" — you take a look at that. You might be right. But you have to be open to following the breadcrumbs. And the resistance is going to be your mind putting something up. So when you look at that, ask: is this coming from the ego? Probably. It's a fear of something. Look at that. Do some analytical work there. But for it to really take, you slowly have to allow yourself to try it. And then it becomes a stronger knowing — what one of my teachers calls strong knowing. The Buddha himself could come and tell me I'm wrong and I'd be like: no, I'm not wrong.

**Shaun:** Right. And almost like experimentation — what if I do follow this intuition? And my experience has been seeing: okay, there is something there. I do feel better after following it. And a lot of those rational fears, even if they came true, weren't as bad as I thought. You build that knowing.

**Faith:** And for me personally, it happens relationally more. Some people can relate to mystery, source, God, in the first person — "I am divine." Which is great. But for me it's more natural to be in relationship with the divine. Praying, being in relational discovery. And it comes back to being yes, I am the divine author of my life, my reality. Or, scientifically, folks like Bernardo Kastrup are coming at it from a completely different direction and arriving somewhere not that different. But the way I look at it is through this perspective of soul. And there's a surrendering that's also important — that you can test little by little, with support from others, from teachers, from your soul, whatever you want.

**Shaun:** Who is supporting you? Going back to the timeline — in the pandemic, where do you find those supports? How are you building this new life?

**Faith:** For me it is literally the gods, the spirits, universal forces. I don't feel alone even when I'm lonely. That kind of thing. Through cultivating that kind of connection, contact with existence — that which is always there, it's eternal, I can always access that. Maybe that's the fruit of some of my inner work.

I'm also part of a lot of relational spaces, because that's edgy for me. I'm not somebody who grew up with a lot of relational connection. Finding, trusting, feeling connected to people is so supportive. We all have the child in us that is hurt and feels alone, right? Nobody's coming back, I'm all alone — that feeling. So relationships — I'm tending to those and really curious about them, taking baby steps. But I feel quite rich in that department now, and I'm thankful for that.

**Shaun:** Yeah, it really helps having people to connect with on this level. That's what I've enjoyed about doing this podcast. So I'm wondering: what is your life now, comparing it to where you started? One thing I think about is your relationship with these different forces — was it something before where you were swept in and not ready, didn't have the knowledge? And now it's more easeful?

**Faith:** Yeah, I'm slowly getting there. You can't rush it. But I know what I'm doing. I know what matters to me and what I'm here to do. So it is not just easeful — I would say blissful. Not in an escapist way. What joy is, is sort of all the emotions being there. And that's accessible to me if I want it at any moment.

Like I said, I started out pretty religious, and there was this punishing God — you must repent, you were born sinful. And it took me a while to realize that's just a very distorted way of looking at things. To come into my own agency around it. I always felt like life just happened to me — I'm not driving anything, so whatever's happening to me is just happening to me. Of course that's not true. And so finding that out. Finding my own power. Owning that. And whatever experiences are yours, mine — actually cherishing those. Everybody wants their experiences to have mattered. And so what it feels like is this joyful sort of discovery process: wow, that thing was really painful. What could that have been? What's the value in it? Why did I have to go through that? And finding that there's something there where before there was only aversion, non-acceptance.

**Shaun:** You can accept everything because it's meaningful. Something behind it.

**Faith:** Yeah. Yeah. Discovering my agency.

**Shaun:** And so what's the agency pointed toward now?

**Faith:** Still in the implementation phase, but I have a specialty I'm wanting to package for the world around soul work. I've done so much study firsthand, and I'm drowning in books. Taking that perspective and elevating it in our culture, into every part of existence. That takes the form of doing work with people, spiritual mentoring. It takes the form of this Soul School project I have — a more relational, initiatory process. Soul initiation. Reclaiming different mythic possibilities — the Heracles version of the hero's journey is definitely there, but there are others that I think are very needed in this time, that are embodied and relational and what I'd call more feminine. Different ones to complement, involving more earth and relational elements. I'm working on my own body of work there. Wanting to get it out with people, and also maybe writing.

**Shaun:** This new orientation, this new purpose — how does it feel compared to before? Do you feel more motivated and committed?

**Faith:** Finding the exact tuning of how it manifests is what I'm doing. Some versions of it are like: aha, this is it. And some versions are: not quite it yet. So that's where I'm feeling around and slowly trying to get my left brain back into: okay, what's the plan, Faith? Not a five-year plan, but a little more structure. That's why I'm in Intentional Ventures.

I do feel more motivated and yes, it's more meaningful. And soul work to me is truly important in terms of what matters in living life. Plus I love it. I love the aesthetic aspect. I have an aesthetic sensibility naturally, and there's a lot of beauty involved in my spirituality. That's the part I'm a little shy about, because there's a part of me that's like: well, what use is that? And then the feeling coming out of me is: but that's what I really love. That's what I see all the time — beauty. Maybe the capacity to perceive that in everybody, in everything, in every moment — maybe that's something to bring forth for others.

Going back to what I was doing before is totally like — you've got to be kidding me, hell no. Even though I had status, I had financial security, it wasn't bad. But it was much easier and much less free. This is much more free. Thank goodness.

**Shaun:** The beauty sounds interesting. Do you have an example of how beauty impacts you and what you do or how you perceive the world?

**Faith:** When they talk about the good, the true, the beautiful — the Platonic transcendentals — beauty is just my way of experiencing things. It's kind of ineffable. I was from very early on immersed in music and art. I was a rigorously trained classical musician. So I have that sense of harmony and voices coming in and out and rhythms changing, and I feel it viscerally. It moves me in my body. Tears are often there.

How does it guide me? I think when we talk about morality, the safest way to think about what's right and wrong is through beauty. Because it's not about this is good, this is evil — not really. It's more like the perfect fit. When you're working on a composition: ah, this fits, this is good. I feel it. And that kind of discernment is superior, I think, to this is right and this is wrong. So I want to go there.

**Shaun:** I love that. When you look at things as good or bad, you usually find: well, there's a bit of good here and bad there depending on perspective. But you can break through that framework and say: it's not perfect either way, but it's beautiful. I can feel that it's harmonious. It's harder to rationalize why. It's just a sense — yes, this is good in the beautiful sense. Let's go with that.

**Faith:** Yeah, exactly. And it matches the intuitive leading — this may not make me money or give me status, but it would be beautiful.

And I think everybody has that quality in their whole life, you know? Like a masterpiece — there are darks here and lights here, and you don't want to throw away certain parts, because then you'll have something with a missing piece. Take it all in. There's a little bit of light there. And it makes for poignancy, it makes for richness in what it really is to be human. My role, I guess, is just to be a champion of that.

**Shaun:** And that might sum up your life — before, you were riding this wave of going from good to bad, out of control. And now you're more of a composer. The good and the bad are still there, but you're weaving them together to make something beautiful.

**Faith:** Yes. Bingo.

**Shaun:** What would you say your beautiful mission is now?

**Faith:** It is what you just said. To see and protect the beauty in everyone, in everything, in every moment. See it — not everyone can. And help people refine what they want, what they think is beautiful. I said before it's bringing more spiritual intelligence. I think we all have it. We've just forgotten. It's very powerful. Helping people revive those capacities, with a specific focus on soul work. Because that's what I've been doing. I can only bring what I've got.

**Shaun:** Yeah. And if people like what you've got, where do they find you?

**Faith:** I have a website — soulschool.co. That's where they can find me, or through the grapevine for now. But that's what I'm bringing into the world. Yeah.

**Shaun:** Amazing. Well, thanks for being on, Faith. It was great learning about you, and what a perspective you've found.

**Faith:** Thank you. I really appreciate this opportunity to think through things and see if you understand. I'm glad we're doing this together.
